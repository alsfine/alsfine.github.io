Understanding Speech When Music Is Blasting

Have you ever tried answering a phone call or speaking to someone in a loud restaurant, at a wedding, or near a concert stage with thumping bass? Our investigation explores how to filter out overwhelming background music so human voices stay natural and intelligible.

The Challenge

Why Is It So Hard to Hear Over Music?

Unlike steady background hum like rain or an air conditioner, music is dynamic, loud, and constantly shifting. More importantly, music bass drums, basslines, and synthesizers vibrate at the exact same pitch range as human vocal cords.

The Ear's Reaction: Acoustic Masking

When loud, booming low-frequency sounds enter the human ear, they trigger an auditory effect known as acoustic masking. The deep bass literally overpowers and drowns out the delicate, higher-pitched sounds of speech consonants (like "s", "t", and "p") that are essential for understanding words.

The Traditional Trade-Off

The "Tin Can" Trap

Standard communication headsets and basic noise filters try to solve this by simply cutting off all sounds below 300 Hz. While this does eliminate deep bass, it creates an unfortunate side effect.

What Gets Lost in the Cut?

A person's natural speaking pitch fundamental sits well below 300 Hz (around 125 Hz for typical male voices and 218 Hz for typical female voices).

Traditional 300 Hz Cut (Natural Voice Retained) ~75% to 84%

Strips up to 26% of vocal warmth, leaving voices sounding thin, metallic, and robotic.

Our Genre-Informed Baseline (Natural Voice Retained) ~98.6%

Leaves the broad voice band open while specifically targeting the music's bass resonance points.

When standard filters amputate that low pitch energy, the person ends up sounding like a thin robot speaking through an empty tin can, and speech recognizers still struggle to make out what was said.

Our Architecture

A Two-Stage Hybrid Approach

Instead of relying on a single blunt filter, our system couples a genre-informed baseline filter with an adaptive noise-canceling algorithm.

IN

Dual-Microphone Input

Two microphones capture sound simultaneously: a Primary Mic near the mouth (voice + room music) and a Reference Mic facing the room (music noise only).

↓ Pre-filters baseline bass
1

Genre-Informed Baseline Filter

Only these static pre-filters are genre-informed. Different music genres put their booming bass in predictable frequency spots (e.g. Techno sub-bass vs. Rock kick-drum punch). This stage gently suppresses those specific resonance areas without gutting the speaker's vocal range.

↓ Feeds to adaptive stage
2

Adaptive Room Tracking (NLMS)

An adaptive algorithm (Normalized Least Mean Squares) continuously calculates how the room's music bounces off walls and subtracts that background noise from the primary signal in real time.

🛡️ Safeguarded by Voice Detector
3

Smart Voice Protection (VAD)

Adaptive filters can accidentally cancel speech if voice leaks into the room microphone. A built-in voice activity detector monitors speech and freezes adaptation while you speak so your voice is never muted.

↓ Delivers clean speech
OUT

Clean Speech Output

The processed signal is delivered to earphones or communications headsets with natural voice warmth intact.

Empirical Evaluation

What the Measurements Showed

To keep our findings objective, we measured our system using standardized engineering metrics rather than subjective listening impressions.

Word Clarity Score (STOI) Standard Metric

Think of this as a test for how distorted the speech sounds. If someone says the word "bat", does it still sound like "bat", or does the noise turn it into "cat" or muffled mush?

A score of 1.0 means crystal-clear speech, while 0.0 means completely unrecognisable.

Heavy Noise (-15 dB) Clarity Gain: +0.06
Raw: ~0.50 → Enhanced: ~0.61 (A modest, helpful boost when background music is loud)
Mild Noise (0 dB) Clarity Gain: Minimal
Raw: ~0.75 → Enhanced: ~0.76 (Speech is already easy to hear; extra filtering is unnecessary)
Word Recognition Percentage (WRS) Practical Test

If an automated speech-to-text listener transcribes 10 spoken words in a noisy room, how many does it get right?

In loud music where an unassisted microphone only caught 2 or 3 words out of 10, our adaptive pipeline helped recover several more words so sentences could actually be understood. Performance varies in practice as sound bounces off walls.

⚖️ Honest Engineering Trade-Offs

Our results are modest and reflect real-world acoustics. While the system demonstrates clear gains in heavy noise, in quiet or mild conditions filtering can introduce slight artifacts.

Additionally, if the speaker's mouth is too close to the secondary room microphone, small amounts of speech leakage can slightly limit maximum noise cancellation.

Hardware Implementation

Running Live on a Low-Power Microchip

This project was not built merely as a simulation on an expensive computer. It runs live in real time on the ESP32-S3 microcontroller sitting on our demonstration table.

Microcontroller Performance

The algorithm executes efficiently within the chip's dual-core processor, leaving ample headroom for other embedded tasks.
